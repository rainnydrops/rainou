Our Rules for the voice leading Link to heading

Augmented 6th chord Link to heading

  • P-function chord
  • chromatic voices to ^5 in two voices
    • these two notes are augmented 6th apart
  • they are voice leading, defined by their relation to V
  • The A6 resolution expands outward. (^b6 to ^5 and ^#4 to ^5)
  • Three type of augmented 6th chord
    • Italian 6th
    • French 6th
    • German 6th
  • Labelling: no inversion or Romain numeral

Build a augmented 6th chord Link to heading

{width=75%}

  • finding two scale degree 5 (root note of V) with chromatic note away
    • Note that the chromatic that goes down must be in the bass
    • The lead is in a < shape
  • add ^1 for stability
  • add the appropriate extra note (Think about the geographic notation where Italian 1 on the bottom, France 2 in the middle and German 3 on top ) (as long as it is semi-tone away, it is good):
    • ^1 for Italian (another tonic)
    • ^2 for french (major second)
    • ^b3 for German (minor third)
      • Note that this will create parallel 5th, so use cadential 64 to avoid parallel.

Other non P-chord type chromatic chord Link to heading

Altered Dominants Link to heading

  • Can be used in anywhere the V would go
  • Variant of V chord which the 5th of the chord (chordal fifth) is either raised or lowered by semi-tone
  • Same rule apply to the minot version

Secondary Mixture Link to heading

  • When the quality is changed but the root of the chord remains the same
  • This is different from Mode mixture where Mode mixture borrows chords from their neighbor keys.
    • Mode Mixture: change the chord by borrowing accidentals from the parallel key. Usually involve raising or lowering the ^3, ^6, ^7 tone
    • Change of the quality based on the root of the chord

{width=50%}

Build a secondary chord mixture Link to heading

  1. Choose a chord from the original diatonic scale
  2. Keep the root the same
  3. Depending on the original chord quality:
    • If Minor: raise the 3rd by semitone -> Major chord
    • If Major: lower the 3rd by semitone -> Minor chord
    • If Diminished: Do one of the Minor/Major Step above + raising the ^5

Exrta notes on Secondary Mixture Link to heading

Note that secondary mixture doesn’t always work, a simple way is just to treat them like non-chord tone (contrapuntal chords)

  • watch out for awkard melodic intervals (dim3/aug2) to add fixes
  • If the secondary mixture duplicates the pre-existing chord type, then they aren’t secondary mixture
  • I, IV, V in Major and i, iv, v in minor will produce the same result as Mode mixture chords.
  • A newly-introduced Major chord whose root moves by P5th down (or P4th up) to the next chord will be a tonicization and therefore cannot be a SM chord
    • e.g. watch out of I ii V, secondary Mixture on ii become II
    • Things become I II V, but II is actually the tonicization of the V, this is understood as a tonicization rather than secondary mixture *exception if the chord that follows them is diminished • It’s extremely rare for tonicizations to be SM since this eliminates the raised ^7 in the temporary key, which is the note that makes us actually hear it as a tonicization (Secondary Mixture can rarely be tonicized)
  • The precedence of secondary mixture come last.

Enharmony Mudulation Link to heading

  • Modulation through respelling the chord enharmonically
  • Enharmony equivalent: sounds identical but is spelled differently
  • Exploit the structure of viio7: The interval between each notes are equal, hence makes it a good canditate for enharmonic modulation
  • Exploit aural similarity between Ger^6^ and V^7^
  • Creates modulation between distantly-related keys
  • Common enhamony mudulation will be using chord of:
    • vii^o7^
    • Ger^6th^
    • V^7^ *Note that the voice leading follows on the newly spelled chord

vii^o7^ to any Modulated vii^o7^ inversion Link to heading

  • Symmetric structure: same interval in between chords
  • The new key can either in major or minor *Note that vii^o7^ has ^7 natural
  • Any Note within vii^o7^ can be choosen as the new root of vii^o7^
    • The new vii^o7^ will be respelled based on the choosen root
    • If the new root is the chordal 7th, this ^4 note need to be repelled to its relative notes (Ab -> G#) Choose any of enhamonic equivalent chord (2-4 measure) to modulate
  • The new vii^o7^ can resolve up by minor second to its new key.

Enharmonic between V^7^ and Ger^6th^ Link to heading

  • The root of the Ger^6th^ (usually ^b6) is the root of V in new key
    • aka The root of the V is the ^b6 in Ger^6th^
  • Count the new key from this new V (down by 5)
  • The new key can either in major or minor

Common Tone Relationships Link to heading

  • Mostly seens in prolongations

  • Contrapuntal/elaborating chord winthin larger prolongation

  • Inversion does not matter

  • The CT chord can comes from the chord before the chord after

  • No traditional Roman numeral

  • There are three different type of common-tone:

    • CT fully/half Diminished 7^th^
      • Fully: all m3^rd^
      • Half: m3, m3, M3 from root
    • CT Mm7^th^ (dominant 7th)
    • CT Aug7^th^

  • Steps to construct a ommon tone

    1. Keep the root of GC as common tone (This is the 7th of the common-tone chord)
    2. Stack thirds below the common tone and add the accidental to form the quality (dim/Mm/Aug) of the chord. Do not modify the CT 7^th^
    3. Rearrange the chord to create smooth motion in each voice (max the leap of third)

  • Note that:

  • The CT does not have to be in the bass

  • CT 7th does not need to resolve down by step (because its contrapuntal chord)

  • CT note can be repeated without a tie

  • The root of the goal chord will always stays the same (no matter where this chord is located)

  • Some CT note can form an already existing chord

Overview Link to heading

Exposition Link to heading

  • Theme1:
    • occurs fully in home key
    • distinct melody
    • tight-knit -> standard phrase model
    • Moderate or fast tempo
    • Could have more than one phrases
  • Bridge (modulating transition)
    • Destabilize the home key and modulate to the subordinate key
    • thematic continuation
    • end in HC in new key
  • Theme2
    • contrast with theme 1
    • Slower, more lyrical than theme 1
    • In subordinate key
    • End with PAC
  • Closing Section
    • Optional section
    • new material after theme 2
    • Two possible theme:
      • Closing Theme
        • Final thematic material before exposition
        • appear to be new thematic (fragmentation)
        • contain full phrase, TSDTs and cadence
      • Codetta
        • Only fragmentation/ or repetitive of motives
        • Consist of harmony prolongation (Post-CE)
        • not a standalone phrase and does not have a cadence

Recapitulation (Similar to Exposition) Link to heading

Bring back the exposition theme material | Similar to exposition | remain in the home key | may eliminate portion of the exposition and replace with new material | Does not transition into new material - retain closing theme or into codetta

  • Theme 1
    • Resolve the series of V brought by development section
    • Often Undergo Structural changes
      • deletion of theme restatements
      • added model+sequence pattern
      • no home-key cadence
  • Bridge
    • Does not modulate
    • tonality unstable via chromatic chord
    • often include tonicization of IV (give a sence of TSD)
    • End in V and into the theme2 (with a series of V at the end)
    • Compress the original bridge material
    • Further develop motives
  • Theme2
    • Occurs in home key
    • loose organization
    • expanded from exposition
    • end with strong cadence in the home key
  • Closing Section
    • Occur independently of a “transition” function
    • May function as a transition to coda
    • Codettas
      • replaced by coda or extended closing section
      • or re-worked to provide more harmony rather than prolongation

Development Link to heading

Technical Details Link to heading

Motivic Elements Link to heading

  • re-uses previous themes
  • Multiple themes may be developed
  • Emphasis on sequences
    • Sequence = repetition with transposition
  • Loosest formal organization of movement
    • No standard phrase

Harmonic Structure Link to heading

  • Avoids authentic cadences in the home key
  • Has a series of modulations, often to “remote” keys
    • based on modal contrast with 2 nd theme
    • end is usually V of home key

Techniques Link to heading

  • Fragmentation and re-combination
  • Variation
    • Different things might happen
  • Re-orchestration
    • Melodies might be moved to other lines
  • More rarely, introduction of new motives
    • Might have new materials

Sections Link to heading

Pre-cores Link to heading

  • Begins with tonic of subordinate key (from end of exposition) • Introduction-like character; more relaxed “feel” than core; features softer dynamics, less rhythmic activity, transitional function • Motivically, often derived from theme 1 or closing theme of EXP • Commonly loose-knit (structured around loose versions of standard phrase models)

Core or Pseudo-core Link to heading

Core Link to heading

  • Core = model + sequence unit (a model + sequence of model)
    • model is commonly 4-8 measures long (often a full phrase)
    • transpositional pattern may change throughout core
    • features fragmentation
  • Possible to have more than one core if motivic material changes
  • “Sturm und Drang” quality
    • Grove Music Online: “artistic aims: to frighten, to stun, to overcome with emotion
    • Minor key • Dynamic extremes & contrast; more use of sfz, crescendo • Counterpoint rather than homophony • Chromaticism • Dramatic contrast • Agitato figures (esp. bass 8ves)
  • Core units often end on V (=HC) in their key, or another type of unstable cadence

Pseudo-core Link to heading

  • No model + sequence technique
  • Still has character of development (eg. Sturm und Drang, etc.)
  • May have improvisatory style
  • Common in Haydn’s development sections, which generally do not use the core/pre-core procedure

Re-Transition Link to heading

• Leads into (and includes) the final standing-on-the-V of the development section • Leads back to home key • Commonly incorporates motivic material from theme 1 • D-prolongation

Framing Sections Link to heading

The Formal Introduction and Coda are Standalone Sections

  • The middle part can be anything large:
    • Rondo: ABACABA
    • Binary or Ternary: AB, ABA, ABA'
    • Sonata
  • Or anything small:
    • Phrase
    • Short as a single bar in the phrase introduction
    • etc

Generally, Intro and Coda Link to heading

• Slow in tempo • Often has a different tempo marking (text or metronome marking) • “Solemn, serious tone” • Least predictable of all large-scale formal units (loose-knit)

Features Link to heading

Formal Introduction Link to heading

  • Sense of anticipation created by:
    • melodic, rhythmic, textural, and dynamic discontinuity
    • instability in harmonic progressions
    • minor mode
    • extensive chromaticism
  • Has qualities associated with French overtures
    • fanfare gestures
    • tutti textures
    • dotted rhythms
    • initial forte dynamic
  • Usually contain their own theme(s)
    • Do not generally modulate, although prominent tonicizations do occur
    • Often end with HC of home key or cadential elision leading into theme 1
  • Distinction: Very short units are normally phrase introductions or codettas, whereas longer units tend to be formal introduction sections or codas
    • Short units often involve harmonic prolongation, little harmonic profile (i.e. no distinct, memorable melody)
    • Longer units may themselves have shorter ones embedded within

Codas Link to heading

  • Distinct from other sections such as the recapitulation
  • Is present to “express the temporal quality of “after-the-end” ”
  • Remains in home key, although may use tonicizations or chromatic sequences
  • In sonatas, begins after the exposition material has all been re-stated (although beginning often obscured). In rondos, begins after the end of the final A section.
  • “the composer can make up for events or procedures that were not fully treated in the main body of the movement”
    • recall main theme ideas
    • present exposition material omitted from the recap
    • re-state ideas from the development
    • shape the overall dynamic line
    • complete a process (eg. wind down from a climax)

Codas vs Codettas Link to heading

  • Codettas: Short and repeated prolongation
    • no phrase
    • no cadence
    • no TSDT, or mini TSDT prolongation
  • Codas: Long and contain its own theme, may include codettas
    • is a phrase
    • has cadence
    • Has full TSDT

Rondo Link to heading

  • Rondo Form: Features motive A that returns repeatedly throughout the piece
    • 5 parts
      • ABACA, B and C are contrasting material
      • ABA(B/C)A
    • 7 parts
      • ABACA(B/D)A
    • Sonata-Rondo

General Features of Rondo Link to heading

  • Main thematic idea alternates repeatedly with contrasting idea
  • Similar to ternary form; can be thought of as an expansion of this form
    • alternation of one expository, returning section with contrasting material
    • often palindromic structure
  • emphasizes contrast!
  • Often used as final movement in symphonies, sonatas, concerti, etc.

Large-scale Feature Link to heading

  • Can involve composite structure (where the A, B, C section itself may contain Binary or Ternary)
  • One section to another via a transition section
  • Coda can be added at the end of the sections:
    • ABABA(Coda)
    • ABACADA(Coda) {width=75%} {width=75%}

Sections Link to heading

Section A Link to heading

  • Tight-knit phrase forms
  • Occurs in the home key
  • Sometimes embellished or truncated, but usually no significant modification to the underlying harmony occurs

Section B & C & D Link to heading

  • Feature significant contrast with A, which may include…
    • motivic design
    • tonality
    • modality
    • rhythm
    • texture/orchestration
  • Not in the home key
  • Usually begin immediately in new key (i.e. uses direct modulation)
  • Are also tight-knit
  • May end with V of home key to smoothly lead back to A tonality

Sonata-Rondo Link to heading

Sonata-Rondo includes the elements of both Sonata and Rondo

Comparing Rondo and Sonata-Rondo form Link to heading

Regular Rondo Sonata-Rondo
A Sections A returns exactly or with only minor variation (same criteria as Ternary form) Repetition of the A section at the end of the exposition is often varied and incorporated into the closing theme material
B sections Repetitions of B are normally in the same key B can appear in two different keys: in the subordinate key the first time through, and the home key the second time through (same as Sonata)
C sections C is usually (new) contrasting thematic material Thematic material from A and B return in C (usually fragmented)

Sections Link to heading

Section A Link to heading

  • Return of A can be significantly varied in sonata-rondo (for example, the A’ is commonly an abridged version of A)
  • Can use a variety of phrase forms (i.e. need not be tight-knit)

Section B Link to heading

  • Features the theme 2 and sometimes a transition
  • Occurs in the subordinate key (i.e. V in Major, III in minor) the first time, and the home key the second time
  • May lack cadential closure

Section C Link to heading

  • Developmental function, and therefore likely to recycle themes from A and B sections
  • May modulate extensively
  • Typically acts like a developmental “core” section, with significant fragmentation, sequencing, and modulation
